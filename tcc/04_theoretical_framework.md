# 4 THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK

        This section presents the theoretical basis to understanding how exchange volatility impacts international trade flows, and how predictive models are used to assess these dynamics. To ensure these answers are well-founded, the discussion draws support from the fields of economics and international relations, as well as from the technological field.

## 4.1 Economic key concepts

        Nominal exchange rate: The nominal exchange rate is the relative price of two currencies and plays a major part in determining international competitiveness due to its impact directly on the price of imports and exports (KIM; LE, 2024). As stated by Kallianiotis (2022, p. 46), "Devaluation increases the price of imports and reduces the price of exports; and due to the law of demand, imports are falling and exports are increasing and the trade account is improved".

        REER: Real Effective Exchange Rate (REER) is a broader-based measure that represents the relative price of goods and services of a country with respect to those of other countries in its trading partner, adjusted for differences in prices while accounting for inflation rates (WAHAB, 2024). It thus offers a better understanding and knowledge of an economy's competitiveness vis-à-vis others since it considers inflation differentials that would, otherwise, either erode or magnify the impact of nominal exchange rate changes (MWANGE et al., 2022).

        Export and Import: Imports and exports are key parts of international trade. Imports represent the purchase of foreign goods or services, while exports are the sales of domestic goods or services abroad. These flows are of key importance for a country's trade balance and general economy (NOTZ; ROSENKRANZ, 2021).

        HS: The Harmonized Commodity Description and Coding System (HS) is a multifunctional international nomenclature that offers a common language for handling classifications by trade sectors, indispensable for recording trade flows at more detailed levels (SCHMIDT; STEINGRESS, 2019). The granularity offered by the HS allows an analysis of how product categories react differently to exchange rate changes, yielding very important sector-specific heterogeneities (SELLAMI et al., 2024).

## 4.2 Models for determining the exchange rate and choosing exchange rate variables: USD/XXX rate and REER

        Various theories have been developed to try to explain those factors affecting the determination of exchange rates; it can be inappropriate to rely on one model to predict, as stated by Mwange (2022, p. 71):

> Even though exchange rates can be confusing, practically everyone has to understand them to function in the modern global economy. As the world gets smaller, there is a greater likelihood that we will have to deal with the risks associated with the fact that there are many different currencies in circulation around the globe and that these currencies will have a direct impact on our world. We must be able to evaluate how changes in exchange rates will impact our financial planning, corporate strategies, governmental initiatives, and other life decisions in order to take the necessary action (both financial and otherwise). Examining various exchange rate theories can help us.

        The analysis of the nominal exchange rate (USD/XXX) is based on traditional methods in the literature, which involves the link between interest rates, inflation and expectations of devaluation as proposed by the International Fisher Effect (IFE), and the relationship that exists between domestic interest rates, on one hand, and foreign rates on other hand, in accordance with Interest Rate Parity (IRP) which explains how exchange rate is connected to equilibrium among money supply as well as demand for it according to monetary approaches. Another relevant theoretical framework is the balance of payments theory, which considers the exchange rate as being determined by the general conditions of foreign transactions and financial exchanges among countries (MWANGE et al., 2022). An assessment based on the U.S. dollar's nominal exchange rate is conspicuous by its superiority as an anchor currency to inform and shape prices internationally even for trade with third countries that do not include the U.S. directly (GOPINATH, G. et al., 2020). As Spring-Ragain (2024, p. 4) states:

> Leading currencies, such as the US dollar (USD) and the euro (EUR), play a central role in the global economy due to their extensive use in international transactions, foreign exchange reserves and financial markets. Their importance lies in several key aspects: as a store of value, where the world's central banks hold substantial reserves in USD and EUR to stabilize their own currencies and manage economic crises; as a medium of exchange, as the dollar and euro are widely used for international trade, facilitating exchanges between countries; and as a unit of account, as many financial contracts, including international loans, are denominated in USD or EUR, reinforcing their central role.

        Thus, the choice of USD/XXX enables a straightforward analysis about the effect of US currency on trade balance.

        The use of the Real Effective Exchange Rate (REER) is justified since it adjusts for inflation differentials and trade structures with various partners, hence reflecting the logic of Purchasing Power Parity (PPP) but going beyond it by addressing its failure to account for shifts in trade flows and bilateral relationships, as discussed by Mwange et al. (2022). The REER is a key variable for macroeconomic analysis in open economies and essential for interpreting the movement of exchange rates and their consequences on economic policy. A careful measurement of the REER, using current weights, is required for a better understanding of exchange rates and also their consequences on economic policy (BAYOUMI et al., 2005). This role of the REER is related to its ability to provide crucial information for policymaking, by deeper understanding of exchange rate movements and their effects in the world economy, particularly during crises (BAYOUMI et al., 2005).

        Therefore, the combined use of the REER exchange rate and the USD/XXX allows for combining both sets of perspectives, which consider the direct impact of the U.S. dollar and global competitiveness adjusted for inflation, thereby improving the predictive power of the analyses to be performed.

## 4.3 Trade flows and sectoral sensitivity

        The structure of bilateral trade flows is a prerequisite for examining the sensitivity of various sectors to exchange rate changes. The detailed bilateral trade data at the Harmonized System (HS) level allows the determination of where imports and exports go, what their value, volume, weight and thus modeling specific sectoral trade relations. This level of granularity enables the analysis on how changes in exchange rates, particularly those of the dollar, affect volumes of trade (GOPINATH, G. et al., 2020). Thus, it is possible to determine areas where trade is more vulnerable or stable in the face of exchange rate shocks.

### 4.3.1 Sectoral impacts of exchange rate fluctuations

        Exchange rate fluctuations affect sectors and products differently, and are considered an important determinant of the competitiveness of goods in international markets (NTHEBE; MOSIKARI, 2025).

        Considering sectoral specificity is fundamental for accurately estimating the elasticity of export and import demand, especially at the macroeconomic level. The specialized literature shows that the exchange rate has had differentiated impacts on prices across various sectors (GODA et al., 2024).

### 4.3.2 Commodities vs. manufactured goods vs high technology

        Within different product types, such as commodities, manufactured goods, and high-tech items, response to the exchange rate vary, as these products have a particular complexity in the global value chain, in the elasticity of demand, and in their cost dynamics (GODA et al., 2024).

        Commodities, for example, are sensitive to exchange rate fluctuations, as they reflect international prices which, in turn, respond directly to variations in the dollar. These factors include production costs and the loss of export competitiveness in a strong currency environment, automatically affecting export incentives and domestic supply in an immediate way (OLIVEIRA et al., 2023). Product complexity and the degree of technological intensity play important roles in determining the sensitivity of sectors to exchange rate fluctuations, with low-complexity sectors in developing countries exhibiting the greatest elasticities in relation to variations in the real effective exchange rate. This is because less sophisticated sectors generally have more flexible production costs and are much more dependent on relative prices to maintain their competitiveness in the international market (GODA et al., 2024).

        From an opposite point of view, Bergin and Corsetti (2020) claim that industries with differentiated goods, with nominal rigidity and costly entry, are less reactive to the exchange rate than members of industries producing very substitutable goods. This sensitivity is reduced by the nature of imperfect competition character and a dependence on preconditions at the stable macroeconomic level.

## 4.4 Macroeconomic control variables

        The Gross Domestic Product (GDP) and the GDP per capita are among the most commonly employed macroeconomic variables for the modeling of trade flows as they track economic development level and the supply and demand capacity of countries. The GDP per capita, which is GDP divided by population, is especially important for measuring wealth disparities between countries and how these might relate to trade patterns. As noted by Wahab (2024), this variable helps explain how similarity, or dissimilarity, in factor endowments can affect bilateral trade patterns, although it presents potential bias problems, such as the accuracy of population data and the inherent limitations of official statistical data.

        Another fundamental variable, conceptually linked to the exchange rate as a dynamic condition, is inflation, which exerts a significant influence on the international competitiveness of products and on the trade balance. For a comprehensive analysis, the examination of the exchange rate regime must also consider external equilibrium in monetary policy, depending on the inflation targeting regime, since flexible rates are effective in correcting external imbalances. Consequently, exchange rate flexibility, a distinctive characteristic of a floating exchange rate regime, can lead to inflationary instability, which, in turn, influences the trade balance (NASIR; VO, 2020).

        Fluctuations in the nominal exchange rate also disrupt international negotiations and directly influence production costs and the competitiveness of tradable goods. While depreciation can increase imports of inputs but also make exporting companies more competitive, exchange rate appreciation can result in lower production costs, reducing import prices of inputs (SANTORO; ROLIM, 2023).

        Financial and economic crises, which tend to accompany sharp exchange rate movements and financial market turbulence, are also expected to affect trade flows through changes in the supply of and demand for goods and services (ARÉVALO et al., 2022).

## 4.5 Time series forecasting models

        The goal of time series analysis is to model and predict the behavior of sequential data, as well as to discover temporal dependencies or patterns. The conventional approach, reflected by econometric models such as ARIMA, assumes that the problem is linear and that the data are stationary so that statistical patterns remain stable over time and structural relationships can be modeled. Preprocessing techniques, such as differencing, are used to make time series data stationary (ABIR et al., 2024).

        Time series analysis is considered basic in the understanding of economic aspects, particularly regarding international trade and networks. It allows the analysis of data at regular intervals in order to identify trends, cycles and patterns that will help to explain the evolution of commercial behavior and the effects of macro events, providing information on past activities, economical dynamics and future performance (YAZAWA, 2023). In this sense, the ability to analyze traffic aims to detect and predict these common temporal patterns in order to make strategic decisions at the levels of international trade and finance (ARÉVALO et al., 2022).

### 4.5.1 ARIMA model

        AutoRegressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) model proposed by Box and Jenkins in the 1970s, is regarded as one of the classic statistical tools for time series analysis and forecasting applications, especially in the finance field (LIN et al., 2020). The model is particularly suitable for the series which have linear trend, stationarity and no seasonality or can be transformed to satisfy these requirements (HE et al., 2020).

        This model has three main parameters: "p," which is the order of autoregression, "d" as the degree of differencing, such that stationarity is reached, and "q", as the moving average order, that allows capturing internal dependencies or removing trends or seasonality in time series (CHATTERJEE et al., 2024).

### 4.5.2 Stationarity tests

        Stationarity is a fundamental subject when applying time-series models, such as ARIMA. The differencing (parameter "d") is used where data demonstrate non-stationarity to transform the series to make it conducive for ARIMA fitting (CHATTERJEE et al., 2024). For the adequacy of time series models, formal tests are performed, such as Dickey-Fuller test, to check the unit root and order of integration process of the series that served as basis for further analysis, such as co-integration (ARÉVALO et al., 2022).

        Non-stationarity of time series, such as trend and seasonality, severely damages the accuracy of models like ARIMA. Hence, the removal of non-stationarity is an important prerequisite for the application of several time-series models like ARIMA, and its elimination or alleviation has been pursued in advanced approaches to improve forecasting performance (LIN et al., 2020).

## 4.6 Machine learning in forecasting

        Recent advances in machine learning have made possible the construction of more resilient and flexible models to forecast time series, which are capable of dealing with non-linearity and complexity that characterize several economic phenomena, surpassing the shortcomings of traditional statistical approaches (CHINN et al., 2023). Machine learning algorithms, like Random Forest and LightGBM, are chosen for this study due to their ability to capture non-linearity and complex relationships within the data without imposing stringent prior assumptions on data distribution or stationarity conditions (CHATTERJEE et al., 2024).

        While Random Forest and LightGBM were not specifically developed for capturing temporal dependency, they are extensively used in time series prediction tasks, due to their flexible ability to model complex relationships and handle high dimensional data. In these applications, time can be combined by feature engineering using the calendar information such as year and month as well as transformation based on the series itself (ZHANG; JIANG, 2025).

### 4.6.1 Random Forest

        Random Forest is one of the most versatile and widely used machine learning algorithms for handling complex data. It is non-parametric, that is, it can deal with different types of responses and predictors over scales and distributions, making it appropriate for high-dimensional setting and complex data. Its power becomes evident from the scale invariance of its frequency-based functions and the fact that its prediction error remains stable even with an increase in the number of trees (HU; SZYMCZAK, 2023). Hence it helps mitigate the overfitting problem present in isolated decision trees since this model works by creating multiple decision trees at training and outputting the class that is the mode of classes (classification) or mean prediction result (regression) of the individual trees (CHATTERJEE et al., 2024). This ensemble learning technique by combining output from many trees reduces variance and bias, maximizing predictive accuracy (LING; WANG, 2024).

        What is more, Random Forest has great practical advantages for applications in economics and business. It is moderately resistant to abnormal points, can cope with multicollinearity between the predictors as well, and is able to generate variable importance measure which further improve applied interpretation. These traits can make the Random Forest a good choice for balancing accuracy and interpretability on empirical scenarios (SALMAN et al., 2024).

        Nevertheless, it is important to recognize some of the methodological weaknesses in the model that were raised by Sellami et al. (2024, p. 4):

> However, while Random Forest models are well suited for analyzing trade data, they have limitations in extrapolation. This inability to extrapolate effectively can hinder their accuracy in predicting future trade flows over time. Furthermore, in general, deep learning regression models tend to outperform Random Forest models.

### 4.6.2 LightGBM

        Otherwise, LightGBM is a gradient boosting framework that shows efficiency and accuracy on large data sets while also being optimized for both training and memory usages (CHATTERJEE et al., 2024). Its architecture can make use of training acceleration techniques and reduce memory usage, which would make it convenient for high-dimensional sparse scenarios. Such innovation significantly contributes to model efficiency when carrying out machine learning duties (GUO et al., 2024).

        Gradient boosting models have been designed to be efficient, and it is considered as an efficient tool for a machine learning problem where computation is a concern and data sizes are very large though some models may behave slowly under huge data size (ENKHBAYAR; ŚLEPACZUK, 2025).

        In real-life scenarios, incomplete data is common, and as demonstrated in studies, techniques such as oversampling with LightGBM manage this data and address class imbalance (GOPINATH et al., 2021).

        While it comes with the advantages of LightGBM, it can be acknowledged that exactly interpreting LightGBM still is a difficult task due to its naturally high complexity in tree boosting models. Thus, although the model may result in highly accurate predictions, it may do this less transparently than simple models, which is pertinent when explainability is vital (GOPINATH et al., 2021).

        Further, the models like LightGBM are also overly sensitive to overfitting, which requires precise adjustments of numerous hyperparameters and careful regularization. The optimization of these hyperparameters, which can be very computationally demanding, is crucial for avoiding overfitting and promoting the model learning to generalize well (SAIED et al., 2013).

        Compared with some other models, which can be so complex that they are not interpretable, LightGBM is usually in a good place on this trade-off. This model is known for its high accuracy as well as its interpretability, making it an effective option when both predictive accuracy and model understanding are important (GUO et al., 2024).

### 4.6.3 Comparison between traditional approaches and ML

        The main difference between machine learning models, like LightGBM and Random Forest, is that they are intrinsically capable of modeling non-linear relationships or complex interactions in the data. Unlike econometric approaches that usually impose strong assumptions on the properties and distribution of data, machine learning models rely much less on premises and adjust more easily to changing data patterns (SELLAMI et al., 2024). This flexibility makes them particularly appropriate for the prediction of complex phenomena, like exchange rates and flows trade, overcoming the constraints and difficulties inherent in traditional methods that do not always reflect the dynamics underlying data (GUO et al., 2024).

        Therefore, a main difference between machine learning and econometric models is that, while in the former the data speaks for itself regarding patterns, in the latter there is a need to make hypotheses based on predetermined model specifications (SILVA et al., 2024).

        On the other hand, in terms of data interpretation, available machine learning models are consistently described as "black boxes", making it difficult to grasp the justification for their predictions compared with traditional econometric models that are a more transparent and built on explicit economic theory (ABOUZAID; BOUSSEDRA, 2025).

### 4.6.4 Hyperparameters and optimization

        Although the "black box" nature of machine learning models presents challenges, interpretability techniques, e.g., SHAP-based methods, are starting to close this divide. These methods, inspired by cooperative game theory, allow for more interpretable examination of how variables influence predictions of the complex modeling task by decomposing predictions into feature contributions and thereby expose non-linear structures and interactions that might have been hidden otherwise (ZHANG; JIANG, 2025). SHAP is known to be very powerful in understanding the importance of each input feature in a model's prediction about a certain case, and in providing an index representing the contribution of each variable to the output value of the model. This capability to assess the relative importance of each feature for a given prediction improves interpretability and trust in machine learning models, even for highly complex models (GUO et al., 2024).

        The use of these methods provides an opportunity for policymakers not only to follow high-fidelity forecast predictions but also to understand the causal reasons behind such forecasts, thus making better and more informed strategic decisions (ZHANG; JIANG, 2025).

        However, identifying the essential hyperparameters for model performance is a major challenge in which slight changes can result in large differences in predictive accuracy (ZHANG; JIANG, 2025).

        Thus, the comparative application of Random Forest and LightGBM in this work gives options to evaluate their predictability on trade flows under a fluctuating exchange rate as a provision to traditional methodologies. The hands-on application of these methods included extensive exposure to computational tools, such as Python and its statistical libraries, data integration tools via SQL, and visualization resources on GitHub for reproducibility with versioning adopted in this study.
