# 4 THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK

        This section presents the theoretical basis required to understand how exchange rate volatility affects international trade flows and how predictive models can be used to assess these dynamics. The discussion draws on two complementary fields: international economics, which provides the substantive concepts of exchange rate, trade flow, and sectoral sensitivity; and applied machine learning, which provides the methodological apparatus for predictive modeling, hyperparameter optimization, and model interpretability.

## 4.1 Economic key concepts

        The nominal exchange rate is the relative price of two currencies and plays a major role in determining international competitiveness due to its direct impact on the prices of imports and exports (KIM; LE, 2024). As stated by Kallianiotis (2022, p. 46), "Devaluation increases the price of imports and reduces the price of exports; and due to the law of demand, imports are falling and exports are increasing and the trade account is improved".

        The Real Effective Exchange Rate (REER) is a broader-based measure that represents the relative price of goods and services of a country with respect to those of its trading partners, adjusted for differences in prices and accounting for inflation rates (WAHAB, 2024). It thus offers a better understanding of an economy's competitiveness vis-à-vis others, since it considers inflation differentials that would otherwise either erode or magnify the impact of nominal exchange rate changes (MWANGE et al., 2022).

        Imports and exports are the two key components of international trade. Imports represent the purchase of foreign goods or services, while exports correspond to the sales of domestic goods or services abroad. These flows are of central importance for a country's trade balance and general economy (NOTZ; ROSENKRANZ, 2021). The Harmonized Commodity Description and Coding System (HS) is a multifunctional international nomenclature that offers a common language for the classification of trade by sector, indispensable for recording trade flows at more detailed levels (SCHMIDT; STEINGRESS, 2019). The granularity offered by the HS allows analyses of how product categories react differently to exchange rate changes, yielding sector-specific heterogeneities of central interest to this study (SELLAMI et al., 2024).

## 4.2 Models for determining the exchange rate and choice of exchange rate variables

        Various theories have been developed to explain the factors affecting the determination of exchange rates, and it can be inappropriate to rely on a single model to predict their movement, as Mwange (2022, p. 71) notes:

> Even though exchange rates can be confusing, practically everyone has to understand them to function in the modern global economy. As the world gets smaller, there is a greater likelihood that we will have to deal with the risks associated with the fact that there are many different currencies in circulation around the globe and that these currencies will have a direct impact on our world. We must be able to evaluate how changes in exchange rates will impact our financial planning, corporate strategies, governmental initiatives, and other life decisions in order to take the necessary action (both financial and otherwise). Examining various exchange rate theories can help us.

        The analysis of the nominal exchange rate (USD/XXX) is grounded in traditional methods of the literature, including the link between interest rates, inflation, and devaluation expectations proposed by the International Fisher Effect, the relationship between domestic and foreign interest rates established by Interest Rate Parity, and the balance of payments theory, which considers the exchange rate as determined by the general conditions of foreign transactions and financial exchanges among countries (MWANGE et al., 2022). The dominance of the U.S. dollar as an anchor currency reinforces the use of bilateral USD/XXX rates as primary modeling variables, since the dollar shapes prices internationally even for trade between third countries (GOPINATH et al., 2020). As Spring-Ragain (2024, p. 4) states:

> Leading currencies, such as the US dollar (USD) and the euro (EUR), play a central role in the global economy due to their extensive use in international transactions, foreign exchange reserves and financial markets. Their importance lies in several key aspects: as a store of value, where the world's central banks hold substantial reserves in USD and EUR to stabilize their own currencies and manage economic crises; as a medium of exchange, as the dollar and euro are widely used for international trade, facilitating exchanges between countries; and as a unit of account, as many financial contracts, including international loans, are denominated in USD or EUR, reinforcing their central role.

        The use of the REER complements the bilateral USD/XXX perspective by adjusting for inflation differentials and trade structures with various partners, reflecting the logic of Purchasing Power Parity while addressing its failure to account for shifts in trade flows and bilateral relationships (MWANGE et al., 2022). A careful measurement of the REER is essential to interpret the movement of exchange rates and their consequences on economic policy, particularly in periods of crisis (BAYOUMI et al., 2005). The combination of REER and USD/XXX in the same predictive framework therefore unites both perspectives: the direct impact of the U.S. dollar and the broader competitiveness adjusted for inflation, improving the predictive power of the analyses to be performed.

## 4.3 Trade flows and sectoral sensitivity

        The structure of bilateral trade flows is a prerequisite for examining the sensitivity of various sectors to exchange rate changes. Detailed bilateral trade data at the Harmonized System level allow the determination of the destination, value, volume, and weight of trade, enabling the modeling of specific sectoral relations. This level of granularity reveals areas where trade is more vulnerable or more stable in the face of exchange rate shocks (GOPINATH et al., 2020).

        Exchange rate fluctuations affect sectors and products differently and are considered an important determinant of the competitiveness of goods in international markets (NTHEBE; MOSIKARI, 2025). Considering sectoral specificity is fundamental for accurately estimating the elasticity of export and import demand, especially at the macroeconomic level: the specialized literature shows that the exchange rate has had differentiated impacts on prices across various sectors (GODA et al., 2024).

        Within different product categories — commodities, manufactured goods, and high-technology items — the response to exchange rate movements varies according to the complexity of the global value chain, the elasticity of demand, and the cost dynamics of each segment (GODA et al., 2024). Commodities are particularly sensitive to exchange rate fluctuations, as their prices are set in international markets and respond directly to variations in the U.S. dollar; the loss of export competitiveness in a strong currency environment automatically affects export incentives and domestic supply (OLIVEIRA et al., 2023). In an opposite direction, Bergin and Corsetti (2020) document that industries with differentiated goods, characterized by nominal rigidity and costly market entry, are less reactive to exchange rate movements than those producing more substitutable goods. Their reduced sensitivity reflects the imperfect competition character of these markets and the dependence on stable macroeconomic preconditions.

## 4.4 Macroeconomic control variables

        Gross Domestic Product (GDP) and GDP per capita are among the most commonly used macroeconomic variables for the modeling of trade flows, since they track the level of economic development and the supply and demand capacity of countries. As Wahab (2024) notes, GDP per capita helps explain how similarities or dissimilarities in factor endowments affect bilateral trade patterns, although its measurement may be affected by the accuracy of population data and the inherent limitations of official statistics.

        Inflation, conceptually linked to the exchange rate, exerts a significant influence on the international competitiveness of products and on the trade balance. Exchange rate flexibility, a distinctive characteristic of floating regimes, can lead to inflationary instability that, in turn, influences the trade balance (NASIR; VO, 2020). Movements in the nominal exchange rate also disrupt international negotiations and directly influence production costs and the competitiveness of tradable goods, with depreciation increasing the relative price of imported inputs while improving the competitive position of exporting firms (SANTORO; ROLIM, 2023). Financial and economic crises, frequently accompanied by sharp exchange rate movements and financial market turbulence, are also expected to affect trade flows through changes in the supply of and demand for goods and services (ARÉVALO et al., 2022).

## 4.5 Time series forecasting models

        The goal of time series analysis is to model and predict the behavior of sequential data, identifying temporal dependencies, trends, and cyclical patterns. The conventional econometric approach, exemplified by the ARIMA model, assumes linearity and stationarity, so that statistical patterns remain stable over time and structural relationships can be modeled with a fixed parametric form. Preprocessing techniques such as differencing are applied to non-stationary series to satisfy these assumptions (ABIR et al., 2024). In the context of international trade, time series analysis enables the identification of trends, cycles, and macroeconomic effects, providing information on past activities and expected future performance (YAZAWA, 2023).

        The ARIMA (AutoRegressive Integrated Moving Average) model, originally proposed by Box and Jenkins in the 1970s, remains one of the classic statistical tools for time series analysis and forecasting, particularly in finance (LIN et al., 2020). It is most appropriate for series that exhibit linear trend and stationarity, or that can be transformed to satisfy these requirements (HE et al., 2020). The model has three main parameters: p, the order of the autoregressive component; d, the degree of differencing required to achieve stationarity; and q, the order of the moving average component, which captures the internal dependencies and removes residual trend or seasonality from the series (CHATTERJEE et al., 2024).

        Stationarity is a fundamental requirement for the application of ARIMA. Differencing (parameter d) is used when the data exhibit non-stationarity to transform the series into a form suitable for ARIMA fitting. Formal tests, such as the Augmented Dickey-Fuller test, are routinely applied to verify the unit root and the order of integration of the series (CHATTERJEE et al., 2024; ARÉVALO et al., 2022). Non-stationarity, in the form of trend or seasonality, severely undermines the accuracy of ARIMA forecasts, and its removal is therefore an essential preprocessing step in any application of the model (LIN et al., 2020).

## 4.6 Machine learning in forecasting

        Recent advances in machine learning have enabled the construction of more flexible and resilient forecasting models, capable of capturing the non-linearity and complexity that characterize many economic phenomena and surpassing the limitations of traditional statistical approaches (CHINN et al., 2023). Random Forest and LightGBM are particularly suited to this study due to their ability to model complex relationships in the data without imposing strong prior assumptions on distributional properties or stationarity (CHATTERJEE et al., 2024). Although neither algorithm was originally developed for capturing temporal dependencies, both are extensively used in time series prediction tasks through feature engineering strategies that incorporate calendar information and lag-based transformations of the series itself (ZHANG; JIANG, 2025).

### 4.6.1 Random Forest

        Random Forest is one of the most versatile and widely used machine learning algorithms for handling complex tabular data. The model is non-parametric: it accommodates different types of responses and predictors across heterogeneous scales and distributions, which makes it particularly appropriate for high-dimensional settings (HU; SZYMCZAK, 2023). Random Forest builds an ensemble of decision trees on bootstrapped samples of the training data and aggregates their outputs through the mean (regression) or the mode (classification) of the individual trees, which mitigates the overfitting characteristic of single-tree estimators and reduces both variance and bias relative to isolated tree learners (CHATTERJEE et al., 2024; LING; WANG, 2024).

        From the standpoint of applied economic analysis, Random Forest offers practical advantages: it is moderately resistant to outliers, copes with multicollinearity among predictors, and produces variable importance measures that support interpretation. These properties make it a sound choice for balancing predictive accuracy and interpretability in empirical research (SALMAN et al., 2024). Nevertheless, methodological limitations should be acknowledged, as Sellami et al. (2024, p. 4) observe:

> However, while Random Forest models are well suited for analyzing trade data, they have limitations in extrapolation. This inability to extrapolate effectively can hinder their accuracy in predicting future trade flows over time. Furthermore, in general, deep learning regression models tend to outperform Random Forest models.

### 4.6.2 LightGBM

        LightGBM is a gradient boosting framework designed to deliver computational efficiency and predictive accuracy on large datasets while remaining optimized for training time and memory usage (CHATTERJEE et al., 2024). Its architecture incorporates training acceleration techniques and reduces memory consumption, which makes it convenient for high-dimensional and sparse scenarios and contributes to its strong performance on tabular learning tasks (GUO et al., 2024). Gradient boosting models are widely regarded as efficient tools for machine learning problems where computational cost is a concern and data sizes are large (ENKHBAYAR; ŚLEPACZUK, 2025).

        In real-world applications, incomplete data is common, and techniques such as oversampling combined with LightGBM have been shown to handle imbalance and missingness effectively in trade-related forecasting (GOPINATH et al., 2021). The interpretability of LightGBM, however, remains a challenge intrinsic to tree boosting models: the model can deliver highly accurate predictions, but it does so less transparently than simple parametric models, which is a relevant limitation when explainability is critical (GOPINATH et al., 2021). LightGBM is also sensitive to overfitting, which requires careful tuning of multiple hyperparameters and adequate regularization; this optimization, although computationally demanding, is essential for proper generalization (SAIED et al., 2013).

### 4.6.3 Comparison between traditional approaches and machine learning

        The principal distinction between machine learning models — such as Random Forest and LightGBM — and traditional econometric models lies in the treatment of functional form. Machine learning algorithms are intrinsically capable of modeling non-linear relationships and complex interactions among variables without imposing strong assumptions on the data, whereas econometric specifications typically rely on predetermined model structures and distributional hypotheses (SELLAMI et al., 2024). This flexibility makes them particularly suitable for the prediction of complex phenomena such as exchange rate dynamics and trade flows, where the dynamics underlying the data may not be adequately captured by linear specifications (GUO et al., 2024). The contrast can be summarized as follows: in machine learning, the data shape the predictive function; in econometrics, the predictive function is shaped a priori and the data inform its parameters (SILVA et al., 2024).

        A persistent limitation of machine learning approaches concerns interpretability. Compared with econometric models, which are built on explicit theoretical assumptions and yield transparent coefficients, machine learning models are frequently characterized as "black boxes" whose internal logic is difficult to inspect (ABOUZAID; BOUSSEDRA, 2025). This limitation has motivated the development of model-agnostic interpretability techniques, discussed in the following subsection.

### 4.6.4 Hyperparameter optimization and model interpretability

        The performance of machine learning models depends critically on the choice of hyperparameters, and small variations in their values can produce substantial differences in predictive accuracy (ZHANG; JIANG, 2025). Systematic optimization techniques are therefore an integral part of any rigorous modeling pipeline. While Grid Search and Random Search remain widely used baseline strategies, more recent frameworks such as Optuna provide algorithm-agnostic Bayesian-style optimization with adaptive sampling, allowing the exploration of complex search spaces with greater efficiency (LING; WANG, 2024).

        On the interpretability side, SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values, derived from cooperative game theory, decompose individual predictions into per-feature contributions, exposing the non-linear structures and interactions that opaque models would otherwise hide (ZHANG; JIANG, 2025). SHAP is particularly powerful for assessing the relative importance of each feature for a given prediction, providing an additive index of contribution and improving both interpretability and trust in complex models (GUO et al., 2024). The combined use of Random Forest, LightGBM, and SHAP-based interpretability tools therefore offers an opportunity for analysts and policymakers not only to obtain high-fidelity forecasts but also to understand the variables driving those forecasts, supporting better-informed strategic decisions (ZHANG; JIANG, 2025).
