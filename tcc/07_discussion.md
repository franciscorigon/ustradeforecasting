# 7 DISCUSSION

        This section interprets the results obtained in this study from two complementary perspectives: a methodological perspective, situated within the practice of Information Systems and applied machine learning, and an economic perspective, contextualized within the empirical literature on exchange rate transmission and bilateral trade flows. The discussion proceeds from the model comparison results toward feature-level interpretability, country-level and sectoral heterogeneity, methodological contributions, acknowledged limitations, and the planned qualitative validation through expert interviews.

## 7.1 Machine learning gains over ARIMA

        The systematic predictive advantage of Random Forest and LightGBM over ARIMA — evidenced by the Diebold-Mariano test in 75% and 83% of series respectively — matches theoretical expectations for non-linear dynamics in trade flows. ARIMA, as a linear autoregressive specification, restricts the relationship between past and future values to a fixed-coefficient combination of lagged terms and disturbances. Tree-based ensembles, in contrast, approximate non-linear interactions among the 73 input features without prior functional assumptions, capturing the joint effects of exchange rates, energy prices, and macroeconomic variables on traded volumes (SONG; CHEN, 2024).

        The prominence of the WTI crude oil price as the most influential feature in the Random Forest model — alongside exchange rate and industrial production variables — illustrates one type of interaction that linear models struggle to represent. Energy price levels and currency dynamics jointly affect commodity-intensive trade corridors in ways that exceed the additive component captured by an autoregressive process. By admitting interactions implicitly through tree splits, both Random Forest and LightGBM exploited information that ARIMA, by construction, could not access.

        The fact that the gap was largest in high-technology series (0.91 percentage points for Random Forest) and manufactured goods (0.46 pp) further supports this interpretation. These categories exhibit heterogeneous demand, sensitivity to global value chain dynamics, and exposure to multiple macroeconomic factors simultaneously — characteristics for which non-parametric models hold a comparative advantage (GOPINATH et al., 2020). Commodities, although still favoring machine learning, displayed the smallest divergence between models, consistent with the comparatively linear pass-through of international prices into traded volumes.

## 7.2 When ARIMA outperforms — eight series and the role of structure

        The result that ARIMA outperformed both machine learning models in 8 of 24 series tempers any sweeping claim of methodological superiority. Inspection of the series in which ARIMA prevailed reveals a consistent pattern: lower observed volatility, near-stationary behavior after differencing, and the absence of structural breaks within the test window. Mexican total exports, with a MAPE of 0.40% under ARIMA, exemplify this profile — a series whose dynamics are well captured by a parsimonious linear autoregressive specification.

        Two methodological factors compound this analysis. First, the ARIMA models in this study were estimated under a walk-forward protocol, generating one-step-ahead forecasts at each test month with access to the most recent realization. Random Forest and LightGBM, in contrast, were configured as direct 36-step forecasters, predicting all 36 test months simultaneously without intermediate feedback. This protocol asymmetry, intentionally documented as part of the methodological scope, structurally favors ARIMA on shorter-horizon predictability.

        Second, the two series flagged by the Ljung-Box test as exhibiting residual autocorrelation — Canadian exports of commodities and Canadian total imports — produced ARIMA errors markedly higher than the model's average. When these mis-specified cases are excluded from the comparison, the average gap between ARIMA and Random Forest narrows to approximately 0.47 percentage points. A properly specified linear model therefore remains a competitive baseline for well-behaved time series, and the question is not whether machine learning is universally superior but under which structural conditions each approach offers meaningful gains.

## 7.3 The Naïve baseline and information asymmetry

        The Naïve baseline's lower MAPE (0.32%) relative to all three models warrants interpretation rather than dismissal. As discussed in Section 6.2, the Naïve forecaster accesses the realized value of the previous month at each step of the test period, while Random Forest and LightGBM operate as direct 36-step predictors without intermediate feedback. This protocol asymmetry, not a methodological defect, accounts for the apparent baseline advantage.

        The implication is twofold. First, the Naïve baseline serves as an upper-bound informational reference, not as a competing forecasting strategy. Second, a 36-step direct forecast and a sequential one-step projection have different operational uses. Trade strategy and macroeconomic planning typically require multi-month lead times incompatible with a Naïve approach that demands the immediately preceding observation. The Random Forest and LightGBM specifications, though structurally penalized in this benchmark comparison, generate forecasts directly applicable to such planning horizons.

        Within the literature on forecast benchmarking, the random walk has been repeatedly identified as a difficult benchmark for monthly macroeconomic series exhibiting strong momentum (BATARSEH et al., 2020). The result obtained here is consistent with this evidence and reinforces the importance of paired statistical tests, such as the Diebold-Mariano employed in this study, when comparing models with structurally different prediction protocols.

## 7.4 Exchange rate as a relevant predictor

        The central research question of this study concerned whether exchange rate fluctuations exert a measurable influence on bilateral trade flows. The SHAP analysis offers strong empirical support for an affirmative answer. In both Random Forest and LightGBM, exchange rate variables — particularly the USD/CAD percentage change — appeared consistently among the top-ranked predictors, alongside the WTI crude oil price and country-specific industrial production indices (KIM; LE, 2024).

        The complementary patterns observed between the two models add interpretive depth. Random Forest assigned greater weight to moving-average representations of exchange rates, with six-month windows particularly prominent, whereas LightGBM placed proportionally higher weight on percentage-change features. Random Forest therefore captured medium-term trend persistence in currency dynamics, while LightGBM responded more sensitively to short-term directional shifts. The convergence of both models in identifying exchange rate variables as relevant predictors — through different functional representations of the same underlying signal — strengthens the finding (GUO et al., 2024).

        This dual confirmation addresses the primary research question framed in Section 1: bilateral exchange rate movements are not a peripheral input but a structurally relevant determinant of monthly trade flow dynamics, observable through model-based interpretability tools. The result is consistent with the literature linking currency volatility to trade competitiveness (RAHMAN et al., 2025; ABIR et al., 2024).

## 7.5 Country-level heterogeneity in exchange rate sensitivity

        The pronounced heterogeneity in FX sensitivity across the three trading partners — Brazil at the higher end, Canada at the lower, and Mexico in an intermediate position — can be interpreted in light of the structural and institutional features of each bilateral relationship. Brazil's elevated sensitivity matches the literature on emerging economies, where less diversified export structures, higher commodity dependence, and the absence of preferential trade agreements with the United States produce greater exposure to bilateral currency fluctuations (GODA et al., 2024).

        Canada's comparatively low FX sensitivity reflects the stabilizing effect of deep productive integration under the United States–Mexico–Canada Agreement (USMCA), supported by the structural complementarity of bilateral supply chains. When exchange rates fluctuate, integrated production processes — particularly in automotive and industrial machinery — exhibit price stickiness and contractual rigidities that dampen the immediate transmission of currency shocks to traded volumes (BERGIN; CORSETTI, 2020).

        Mexico's intermediate position is consistent with its hybrid integration profile: USMCA participation provides partial insulation analogous to that observed for Canada, while the maquiladora-driven manufacturing structure and substantial commodity component retain residual sensitivity to exchange rate movements. The country-level pattern observed here is consistent with both economic theory on regional trade integration and the empirical literature on differential exchange rate pass-through across trading partners.

## 7.6 Sectoral patterns in exchange rate transmission

        The sectoral disaggregation revealed a consistent ordering of FX sensitivity: commodities at the higher end, manufactured goods in an intermediate position, and high-technology products at the lower end. The pattern was stable across all three country pairs and accords with the literature on heterogeneous exchange rate transmission across product categories.

        The high sensitivity of commodity flows reflects the predominance of USD-denominated international pricing in markets such as crude oil, soybeans, and iron ore. When the dollar appreciates or depreciates against a partner currency, traded volumes adjust through arbitrage and substitution effects with limited domestic insulation (OLIVEIRA et al., 2023). This finding is consistent with the structural pricing mechanisms documented in the commodity economics literature.

        The lower sensitivity of high-technology products reflects the differentiated nature of these goods, the prevalence of long-term contracts, and the integration of trade flows within global value chains. Bergin and Corsetti (2020) document that relationship-specific investments and bilateral pricing arrangements partially insulate differentiated goods from short-run currency fluctuations, and the same mechanism appears to operate in the U.S. trade with Canada, Mexico, and Brazil. Manufactured goods occupied an intermediate position, reflecting the coexistence of standardized inputs — more exposed to exchange rate arbitrage — and differentiated components traded within integrated supply chains.

## 7.7 Methodological contributions

        Beyond the findings on exchange rate sensitivity, this study offers several methodological contributions for Information Systems research applied to economic forecasting. The first is the implementation of a complete CRISP-DM pipeline across all six phases, applied to monthly macroeconomic forecasting with multiple country pairs and sectoral granularity (SHIMAOKA et al., 2024; MARTÍNEZ‐PLUMED et al., 2019). The framework provided a process backbone for integrating heterogeneous data sources, reproducible feature engineering, and systematic model evaluation.

        The second contribution is the systematic application of SHAP values to both Random Forest and LightGBM, enabling a comparative interpretability analysis beyond the typical practice of analyzing only the best-performing model. By contrasting the relative weights assigned to moving-average and percentage-change FX features across the two algorithms, the analysis revealed complementary perspectives on the same economic phenomenon (SUDJIANTO; ZHANG, 2024). This dual-model approach reduces the dependence of the conclusions on the inductive bias of any single learner and aligns with the literature on interpretable machine learning for policy-relevant forecasting (KHAN et al., 2024).

        The third contribution concerns the statistical evaluation framework. The use of the Diebold-Mariano test with the small-sample correction proposed by Harvey et al. (1997) provides statistically rigorous pairwise comparisons that do not rely on aggregate metric averages. The test exposed the predictive hierarchy that the non-significant Friedman result alone would have obscured, and the application of multiple complementary tests (Friedman, Wilcoxon, and Diebold-Mariano) follows the standard practice in modern forecasting literature.

        The fourth contribution is reproducibility. The entire pipeline — source code, processed datasets, trained models, and evaluation outputs — is organized in a public repository with versioned notebooks. Reproducibility, often neglected in applied economic research, is a central methodological requirement in Information Systems and was treated here as a first-class deliverable rather than a documentation afterthought (VRANJEŠ; NIGGEMANN, 2024).

## 7.8 Limitations

        Several limitations should be noted to qualify the interpretation of the results. The sectoral target series were estimated by combining annual HS2 sectoral proportions with monthly aggregate flows, an approximation that introduces systematic distortion in periods of structural change within a given year. The effect is most pronounced for the Commodities category, where intra-annual price volatility and shipment lumpiness can deviate from the annual proportional structure. Future work could replace this approximation with monthly HS2 disaggregated series once they become available at sufficient coverage.

        The statistical comparison was conducted across 24 series, a sample size that constrains the discriminatory power of the Friedman test. The non-significant Friedman result (p = 0.417) should not be read as evidence of model equivalence; the Diebold-Mariano test, applied to the underlying error sequences rather than to aggregate MAPE values, recovered the predictive hierarchy with statistical significance. Combining both tests is therefore necessary to avoid mischaracterizing the comparative performance of the models.

        The test window (January 2022 to December 2024) overlaps with major macroeconomic disturbances: the post-pandemic recovery, the energy price shock following the 2022 Russia–Ukraine conflict, and the subsequent monetary tightening cycle in the United States. Although these disturbances are part of the realized empirical record, they may produce performance estimates not fully representative of more stable macroeconomic regimes. Extending the analysis to additional out-of-sample windows would help characterize the regime sensitivity of the comparative results.

        A further methodological constraint concerns the prediction protocol. The Random Forest and LightGBM models were configured as direct 36-step forecasters without intermediate feedback, while ARIMA followed a one-step walk-forward procedure. Although intentional and documented in the methodology, this asymmetry favors ARIMA on shorter horizons and the Naïve baseline in the immediate-step comparison. A future iteration of the pipeline could implement recursive multi-step machine learning forecasts with sequential retraining to provide a fully symmetric comparison across protocols.

        Finally, the qualitative validation through interviews with practitioners in international trade and machine learning, originally proposed in TCC1, was planned but not yet conducted at the time of writing. The discussion of the results in this study is therefore based exclusively on the quantitative outputs of the modeling pipeline, with the qualitative dimension addressed in the following subsection as a planned complement.

## 7.9 Planned qualitative validation

        To complement the quantitative findings, the original research design included semi-structured interviews with practitioners in two domains: international trade and machine learning applied to economic forecasting. The interviews were planned to be conducted before the final submission of this work and are intended to provide a qualitative dimension to the discussion of the results.

        The expected contribution of the interviews is twofold. From the international trade perspective, practitioners are expected to assess whether the country-level and sectoral sensitivity patterns identified by the SHAP analysis correspond to operational expectations and observed market behavior in the U.S. trade relationships with Canada, Mexico, and Brazil. From the machine learning perspective, practitioners are expected to evaluate the appropriateness of the methodological choices — feature engineering, validation protocols, and interpretability instruments — relative to current practice in production forecasting systems.

        Once conducted, the interview findings will be incorporated into this section to triangulate the quantitative results with practitioner judgment, in line with the methodological orientation of Information Systems research toward the validation of artifacts in their use context.
