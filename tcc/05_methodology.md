# 5 METHODOLOGY

        This study adopted CRISP-DM (Cross-Industry Standard Process for Data Mining) as its guiding methodological framework (SHIMAOKA et al., 2024; MARTÍNEZ‐PLUMED et al., 2019), a technology-independent process model structured into six iterative phases: Business Understanding, Data Understanding, Data Preparation, Modeling, Evaluation, and Deployment. Although the phases are presented sequentially in the subsections that follow, the actual research process involved repeated cycles between Data Preparation and Modeling, and between Modeling and Evaluation, consistent with the iterative nature of the framework. The Modeling and Evaluation phases receive greater methodological detail in line with their analytical centrality to the comparative experiment.

## 5.1 Business Understanding

        The research problem addressed in this study concerns the predictive influence of exchange rate fluctuations on bilateral trade flows between the United States and three major partners — Canada, Mexico, and Brazil — at monthly frequency and at the level of broad sectoral aggregates. From the perspective of Information Systems and Data Mining, the problem was operationalized as a comparative forecasting task in which three algorithmic families — a linear autoregressive baseline (ARIMA), a bagging ensemble of decision trees (Random Forest), and a gradient boosting ensemble (LightGBM) — were evaluated against a common set of 24 target series and a shared collection of 73 input features.

        The Data Mining objective was twofold: to assess the relative predictive performance of the three algorithms across the 24 series, and to characterize the marginal contribution of exchange rate variables through model-agnostic interpretability tools. The success criteria were defined a priori as statistically significant pairwise differences in forecast error (Diebold-Mariano test) combined with a coherent attribution pattern of the exchange rate features through SHAP values across the two machine learning models.

## 5.2 Data Understanding

        Three data sources were integrated to construct the analytical dataset. The Federal Reserve Economic Data (FRED) repository provided the daily bilateral exchange rate series (USD/CAD, USD/MXN, USD/BRL), the real effective exchange rate (REER) indices for the four economies, and the macroeconomic control variables (Federal Funds Rate, U.S. industrial production, Consumer Price Index, GDP per capita, and the WTI crude oil price). The United Nations Comtrade database supplied the bilateral trade flows at the HS2 sectoral level, with annual data covering 2000–2024 and monthly data covering 2010–2024. The World Bank Open Data API contributed annual indicators on partner-country macroeconomic conditions, used as supplementary variables.

        Although data collection extended back to 2000 to maximize the potential coverage of post-Global Financial Crisis dynamics, the modeling window was restricted to 2010–2024 to align with the availability of monthly Comtrade data, yielding 180 monthly observations. The 24 target series were defined along three dimensions — country (Canada, Mexico, Brazil), direction (exports, imports), and sector (commodities, manufactured goods, high-technology, total) — producing the structured 3 × 2 × 4 evaluation design used in the subsequent phases.

        An exploratory data analysis of the collected series, including time series plots, distributional summaries, and bivariate correlations, was conducted at this stage to inform the feature engineering and outlier handling decisions of the Data Preparation phase. The detailed exploratory artifacts are reported in Appendix B.

## 5.3 Data Preparation

        The Data Preparation phase combined frequency alignment, statistical transformation, outlier treatment, and feature engineering into a reproducible pipeline implemented in the `03_data_preparation.ipynb` notebook. Daily exchange rate series were aggregated to monthly frequency by simple mean; quarterly GDP series were forward-filled to a monthly cadence to align with the target frequency. Missing values introduced by weekends and holidays in the daily exchange rate data were resolved through forward-fill prior to the monthly aggregation step.

        Two parallel target representations were generated to accommodate the heterogeneous statistical assumptions of the three modeling families. The first applied a natural logarithm transformation to the trade flow series, producing the log-transformed variant used by the Random Forest and LightGBM models. The second applied an additional first difference to the log-transformed series, producing the differenced variant used by ARIMA. Stationarity of the differenced series was confirmed at the 5% significance level through the Augmented Dickey-Fuller test for the majority of the 24 series.

        Outliers in the feature space were identified through the interquartile range criterion and retained in the dataset, complemented by binary regime indicators for the Global Financial Crisis (September 2008–June 2009) and the COVID-19 pandemic (March 2020–June 2021). Feature engineering generated lagged variants of the macroeconomic controls (1, 3, 6, and 12 months), rolling moving averages of the exchange rate and trade variables (3, 6, and 12 months), monthly percentage change transformations, and calendar-based indicators (month and quarter). The final analytical dataset comprised 180 monthly observations across 97 columns, of which 73 corresponded to input features and 24 to target representations.

        The dataset was split chronologically into a training window (January 2010 to December 2021, 144 months) and an out-of-sample test window (January 2022 to December 2024, 36 months). The chronological split preserved the temporal ordering of the observations and avoided look-ahead bias, in line with the recommendations of the time series forecasting literature (KHAN et al., 2024; MORTEZANEJAD; WANG, 2025). After accounting for the 12-month lag features, the effective Random Forest and LightGBM training window comprised 132 observations; for ARIMA, the effective window was 143 observations following the first-difference transformation.

## 5.4 Modeling

        The Modeling phase produced 72 forecasting models — the factorial combination of 24 target series and 3 algorithmic families — supplemented by a Naïve baseline that served as an informational reference. All models were trained independently per series using the shared set of 73 country-pair-agnostic features, allowing direct comparison across countries and sectors. The implementation is documented in the `04_modeling.ipynb` notebook.

        The ARIMA models were fitted to the differenced logarithmic series under a walk-forward validation protocol. For each of the 36 test months, the model was re-estimated on the cumulative training data up to that point, generating a one-step-ahead forecast that was subsequently appended to the training window for the next iteration. Order selection followed an automated grid search over p ∈ {0, 1, 2, 3}, d ∈ {0, 1}, and q ∈ {0, 1, 2, 3}, with the optimal (p, d, q) combination selected by minimum Akaike Information Criterion on the in-sample residuals. The dominant optimal order across the 24 series was (3, 0, 3).

        The Random Forest and LightGBM models were configured as direct 36-step forecasters operating on the logarithmic target representation. Each model was trained once on the 132-observation training window and generated all 36 test-period predictions in a single pass. Hyperparameter optimization was performed with Optuna (AKIBA et al., 2019) over 30 trials per series, using a TimeSeriesSplit cross-validator with 5 folds to preserve temporal ordering during the inner search (RASCHKA et al., 2020; LING; WANG, 2024). The Random Forest search space covered the number of estimators, maximum tree depth, minimum samples per leaf, and the maximum-features ratio. The LightGBM search space additionally included the learning rate, number of leaves, subsampling and column-subsampling fractions, and the L1 and L2 regularization terms. The use of TimeSeriesSplit during hyperparameter selection followed the time series cross-validation guidance of Abir et al. (2024) and the broader recommendations on overfitting control in temporal data (WANG; XIE, 2024).

        The Naïve baseline was implemented as a random-walk forecaster, defined as log(y_t) = log(y_{t-1}) for the logarithmic target. The baseline accessed the realized value of the previous month at each step of the test window, a structural information advantage over the direct 36-step machine learning forecasters; the implications of this asymmetry are discussed in Section 6.2 and Section 7.3.

        All trained models, hyperparameter configurations, and per-series forecast outputs were serialized to disk for downstream evaluation. Model objects were saved as Python pickle files in the `models/` subdirectory; numerical forecast outputs and per-model metric tables were stored as comma-separated value files in the `results/forecasts/` subdirectory, ensuring full reproducibility of the comparative experiment.

## 5.5 Evaluation

        The Evaluation phase, implemented in the `05_evaluation.ipynb` notebook, combined point-forecast metrics, residual diagnostics, statistical significance tests, and model-agnostic interpretability instruments (SATRI et al., 2023). Point-forecast accuracy was assessed through three complementary metrics: the Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), the Mean Absolute Error (MAE), and the Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE), following the standard practice in applied forecasting research (AYITEY et al., 2023). The MAPE was adopted as the primary headline metric due to its scale invariance across the 24 target series.

        The adequacy of the ARIMA specifications was verified through the Ljung-Box test applied to the in-sample residuals at lag 10, with the null hypothesis of white-noise residuals evaluated at the 5% significance level. Series for which the null was rejected were retained in the comparative evaluation but flagged as cases of potential model mis-specification in the discussion of the results.

        Three statistical tests were applied to the comparative forecast errors. The Friedman test was used as a non-parametric omnibus comparison of the three algorithms across the 24 series, treating each series as a block. Pairwise comparisons were then conducted with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test under Bonferroni correction for multiple testing, and with the Diebold-Mariano test using the small-sample correction proposed by Harvey et al. (1997). The Diebold-Mariano test, which operates on the underlying error sequences rather than aggregated metric averages, served as the primary instrument for ranking the three algorithms in the comparative analysis.

        Model interpretability was assessed through SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values computed via the TreeExplainer algorithm of the `shap` library (LUNDBERG; LEE, 2017; GUO et al., 2024; SUDJIANTO; ZHANG, 2024). SHAP values were generated independently for the Random Forest and the LightGBM models, enabling a comparative analysis of the feature attribution patterns across the two algorithmic families. A composite exchange rate sensitivity metric was constructed as the mean absolute SHAP value of the exchange rate and REER features, averaged between the two machine learning models, and visualized as a heatmap across the 24 country-sector combinations to support the sectoral and country-level interpretation of the results.

## 5.6 Deployment

        Although deploying a production-grade forecasting service was outside the scope of this academic study, the Deployment phase of CRISP-DM was operationalized through the publication of a reproducible artifact set. The complete pipeline — raw and processed datasets, feature engineering scripts, trained model objects, hyperparameter configurations, forecast outputs, and evaluation notebooks — was organized in a public Git repository with versioned commits (SHIMAOKA et al., 2024). Reproducibility was treated as the primary deployment deliverable, supporting independent verification and extension by future research.
