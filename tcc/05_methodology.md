# 5 METHODOLOGY

        In this research, CRISP-DM (Cross-Industry Standard Process for Data Mining) was used to predict the impact of exchange rate changes on trade flows between the United States and Canada, Mexico, and Brazil. The decision was motivated by the need to connect macroeconomic data, time series and predictive models.

        CRISP-DM is widely recognized as one of the most used methodologies in data mining projects, organizing the project into well-defined phases. Its structure guarantees the quality and transparency of models at all stages, from business understanding to deployment, enabling insights and analytical solutions (TRIPATHI et al., 2021).

        The process consists of six phases addressed in this chapter: Business Understanding, Data Understanding, Data Preparation, Modeling, Evaluation and Deployment (WIRTH; HIPP, 2000). Although presented sequentially, the actual research involved repeated cycles between Data Preparation and Modeling, and between Modeling and Evaluation, consistent with the iterative nature of the framework.

## 5.1 Business Understanding

        This is the first phase of CRISP-DM, where project goals are set. Requirements are transformed into a definition of the data mining problem and project plans (SHIMAOKA et al., 2024). It also identifies external forces such as exchange rate volatility and macroeconomic shocks, which affect both analytical models and the interpretation of results (NGALAWA; KUTU, 2017).

        To ensure that the forecasting results are usable for strategic decisions, knowledge of the business objectives was necessary. This comprehension mapped the influence of trade flows and currency variations, and identified relevant actors such as stakeholders, policy and decision makers. The phase also defined which economic aspects deserve deeper analysis, including sectoral sensitivity, export-import variation and the effect of macroeconomic incentives on trade flow decisions (BATARSEH et al., 2020).

        In this study, the Business Understanding phase determined the most important macroeconomic variables, defined performance metrics, and stated the core problem. The global purpose was to assess Random Forest and LightGBM compared to ARIMA when predicting trade flows between the United States and Canada, Mexico and Brazil from 2000 to 2025 under exchange rate fluctuations. The choice of variables and metrics came from the theoretical review of ARIMA limitations on complex macroeconomic series and the potential of ML to model nonlinear relationships (LIN et al., 2020).

        Business Understanding structured the remaining stages to face the specific research objectives: identifying the sectors most vulnerable to exchange rate changes, analyzing how these effects vary across time horizons, evaluating each model's predictive power, and examining the role of macroeconomic factors in trade flow volatility.

## 5.2 Data Understanding

        The Data Understanding phase describes, explores and checks the quality and availability of data, becoming the basis for future transformations. The raw data is collected and analyzed to recognize patterns, identify limitations and formulate hypotheses, underpinning the success of experiments (RIESS, 2022).

        The data for analysis and prediction usually consists of historical exchange rate data from financial markets or central banks, plus macroeconomic indicators such as interest rates, inflation, GDP growth, trade balance and FDI from sources such as the World Bank, International Monetary Fund or national statistical bureaus (ABIR et al., 2024). In this analysis, this included USD/CAD, USD/MXN and USD/BRL exchange rates from central banks and the Federal Reserve (FED), bilateral trade flows from UN Comtrade and the U.S. Census Bureau, macroeconomic indicators from the World Bank and OECD, and sectoral data on commodities, manufactured goods and high-tech to capture sectoral asymmetry.

        The data frequency ranges from monthly to quarterly, depending on series availability. This flexibility was essential to adjust models to specific time horizons and to understand both short-term effects and long-term structural activities (ABOUZAID; BOUSSEDRA, 2025). Data manipulation and integration used Python with Pandas and NumPy for loading and processing (BATARSEH et al., 2020).

        During the early stage of exploration, descriptive statistics such as mean, median and standard deviation are computed to understand distribution and relationships between variables (LI et al., 2025), guiding the cleaning and transformation procedures required for reliable analyses (VERSTRAETE et al., 2021).

        The data covered the period from 2000 to 2024 at multiple frequencies, daily for exchange rates and monthly for macroeconomic variables and trade flows. The modeling window was restricted to 2010–2024 by the availability of monthly Comtrade data, yielding 180 monthly observations. The 24 target series combined three countries (Canada, Mexico, Brazil), two directions (exports and imports) and four sectors (commodities, manufactured goods, high-technology and total), producing the 3 × 2 × 4 design used in subsequent phases. Detailed exploratory artifacts are reported in Appendix B.

## 5.3 Data Preparation

        After collection, the Data Preparation phase ensures the quality and suitability of raw data, guaranteeing it is cleaned, transformed and properly formatted. This includes correcting errors and inconsistencies, treating missing values, recoding data types, and normalizing or standardizing variables for use in model building. Models like ARIMA, Random Forest and LightGBM require careful data treatment for optimized performance and valid predictions. As mentioned by MA et al. (2024), the adequacy and quality of input data are essential indicators of the success and reliability of model results.

        Screening variables is important to retain only features that contribute to prediction and performance (MA et al., 2024). Macroeconomic variables such as exchange rates, GDP, interest rates, exports, imports and trade sectors are important factors in studies of economic dynamics and international trade.

        Integrating several databases was necessary to create a unified dataset for modeling. Frequency conversion was essential for adjusting ARIMA, which requires temporal consistency (BRYAN et al., 2023). Standardization was essential to make results comparable and valid (ABIR et al., 2025).

        Outlier detection and processing, which identifies atypical observations, is important because untreated outliers may mislead statistical results and prediction accuracy (TAJMOUATI et al., 2022). Outliers were identified using descriptive statistical methods, including the interquartile range and visual inspection of graphs (SUDJIANTO; ZHANG, 2024). Automatic exclusion is not generally recommended; including outliers can be reasonable for checking model robustness, rather than treating them as invalid data (DIAS et al., 2020).

        Differentiation is widely used in time series analysis, especially in econometric models where trends and seasonality must be removed to stabilize the series mean (LIN et al., 2020). Data Preparation also includes feature engineering, generating and transforming variables (CHOWDHURY et al., 2024). Transformations such as lags, moving averages and percentage variations capture temporal behavior for econometric and ML models (LIN et al., 2020).

        In this study, the Data Preparation pipeline aggregated daily exchange rate series to monthly frequency by simple mean, and forward-filled quarterly GDP series to monthly. Two target representations were generated: a log-transformed variant for Random Forest and LightGBM, and a log-differenced variant for ARIMA. Stationarity of the differenced series was confirmed at the 5% level through the Augmented Dickey-Fuller test for most series. Outliers identified by the interquartile range were retained, complemented by binary indicators for the Global Financial Crisis (2008–2009) and the COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2021). Feature engineering generated lags (1, 3, 6 and 12 months), moving averages (3, 6 and 12 months), monthly percentage variations and calendar indicators. The final dataset contained 180 monthly observations across 97 columns, 73 features and 24 target representations. The chronological split used January 2010 to December 2021 for training and January 2022 to December 2024 for the out-of-sample test, preserving temporal order and avoiding look-ahead bias (VERSTRAETE et al., 2021).

## 5.4 Modeling

        The Modeling phase, following Data Preparation, applies statistical tests and machine learning algorithms iteratively to construct, select and refine predictive models that detect patterns in the data (CHOWDHURY et al., 2024; MA et al., 2024). In this paper, both econometric models like ARIMA and machine learning models such as Random Forest and LightGBM were used and compared to take advantage of their time series forecasting ability.

        The modeling approach in time series prediction involves several steps: defining and preparing features for a supervised model; finding a prediction window; splitting data into training and test sets to assess generalization; tuning hyperparameters through cross-validation; comparative evaluation based on metrics; and explainability analysis (SILVA; TIMO, 2022). Avoiding data leakage, the so-called "contamination zone" effect, is important because inconsistency between training and testing phases can lead to erroneous assessments of model performance (REMIL et al., 2024).

        The way to define time series models as inputs is varied, and ARIMA models are widely used for univariate time series (PORTO, 2022). For Random Forest and LightGBM, decision tree-based models, the input can be multivariate and does not require stationarity. This flexibility allows incorporating macroeconomic lagged variables (BOTZ et al., 2025) and features such as lags, moving averages and calendar features, which capture temporal and seasonal dynamics (ZANOTTI, 2025).

        The ARIMA (AutoRegressive Integrated Moving Average) model has been used as a benchmark for time series analysis, including financial and economic series. Its approach removes non-stationarity through differentiation, enabling modeling (LIN et al., 2020). Its parameterization (p, d, q) models autoregressive dependencies (p), differentiation degree to remove trends (d), and moving average component (q) (RIZVI, 2024). ARIMA has been used as a fundamental baseline for comparative forecasting studies, and in some applications it can emerge as the best approach for short-term forecasts (BOTZ et al., 2025).

        For each univariate time series, modeling practice started with a stationarity test (Augmented Dickey-Fuller, ADF) to determine the differentiation order (d) of the series. Final model selection used information criteria such as the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), identifying the most parsimonious and best-fitted models. Residual diagnostics confirmed the absence of heteroscedasticity and autocorrelation (White Noise properties), validating model assumptions (REICHERT; SOUZA, 2020). The backtesting process used a sliding window approach, with models trained on historical data up to a point in time and predictions generated for the following period (BOTZ et al., 2025).

        Random Forest is implemented through an ensemble that aggregates multiple decision trees over the supervised matrix (ABIR et al., 2025). Unlike ARIMA, it handles non-linear relationships and complex interactions between predictors without assuming stationarity (CHATTERJEE et al., 2024). It tolerates various data types and is less sensitive to outliers and multicollinearity, an advantage for macroeconomic and financial data (LING; WANG, 2024; SALMAN et al., 2024).

        Similar to Random Forest, Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM) is a powerful time series forecasting algorithm, known for speed and accuracy on large, high-dimensional datasets (ZANOTTI, 2025). It is also based on a supervised matrix, but requires caution in hyperparameter optimization and treatment of missing values (MORTEZANEJAD; WANG, 2025).

        Nevertheless, well-tuned machine learning models for financial market prediction should be treated with caution due to uncertainty about their generalization across market conditions, requiring thorough sensitivity analyses to validate stability. The risk of overfitting must also be considered to ensure effectiveness in real-world scenarios and durability across historical periods (WANG; XIE, 2024).

        Temporal validation is key in time series analysis because it maintains the order of data, avoids information leakage from future to past, and ensures evaluation under realistic forecasting settings (MORTEZANEJAD; WANG, 2025). To minimize improper evaluation and over-generalization, series should be strictly split in chronological order (KHAN et al., 2024), preserving the temporal structure and preventing access to future data during training (KHAN et al., 2024; SAFAROV et al., 2025). This approach uses backtesting based on sliding window methods, known as time series cross-validation (TS-CV), which enables iterative evaluation while preserving chronological order (ABIR et al., 2024).

        In machine learning, hyperparameter tuning is essential for maximizing performance, with various techniques applied (VRANJEŠ; NIGGEMANN, 2024). Common methodologies include Grid Search, Random Search and Optuna, which trains a probabilistic model relating hyperparameters to performance metrics. Each methodology has characteristics that affect efficiency and the scope of the search space (RASCHKA et al., 2020; LING; WANG, 2024).

        The Modeling phase produced 72 forecasting models from the combination of 24 target series and 3 algorithmic families, plus a Naïve random-walk baseline. ARIMA models were fitted under a walk-forward protocol, with order selection by AIC grid search over p from 0 to 3, d from 0 to 1, and q from 0 to 3. Random Forest and LightGBM were configured as direct 36-step forecasters on the logarithmic target, with Optuna performing hyperparameter optimization (AKIBA et al., 2019) over 30 trials per series using a TimeSeriesSplit cross-validator with 5 folds. The Naïve baseline used the last observed log value of the series; its structural information advantage over the 36-step ML forecasters is discussed in Sections 4.2 and 5.3.

## 5.5 Evaluation

        In CRISP-DM, the evaluation phase verifies that the developed models meet the business objectives previously defined and are sufficiently tuned for deployment (SATRI et al., 2023). Model validation is achieved by scrutinizing performance, interpretability and robustness metrics, confirming credibility and utility for decision-making (SUDJIANTO; ZHANG, 2024).

        To compare models, predictive accuracy and practical relevance are assessed using metrics such as the Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE), frequently used to gauge prediction accuracy (AYITEY et al., 2023). These measures compare machine learning models among themselves and to ARIMA, identifying the most effective and quantifying the value added by complex models over baselines (UDDIN et al., 2025).

        Although ML-based models may provide superior performance, their interpretability is often difficult to achieve, particularly in financial applications where transparency is a necessity (SUDJIANTO; ZHANG, 2024). To address this opacity, interpretability methods such as feature importance analysis and SHAP-based explainers are applied, elucidating the contribution of each input variable to the model's predictions (GUO et al., 2024). This allows policymakers and financial analysts to understand not only "what" is predicted, but also "why". Overcoming the opacity of "black boxes" is crucial for trust and adoption of these predictive tools (ABOUZAID; BOUSSEDRA, 2025), and is essential for policymakers and investors to understand the forecasting logic and make informed decisions (KHAN et al., 2024).

        Robustness tests and sensitivity analysis verify the validity and performance of a model across various market conditions (SUDJIANTO; ZHANG, 2024), providing systematic assessments of stability under different conditions, adaptability to simulated shocks and extreme events, and resilience during high-volatility periods (ABOUZAID; BOUSSEDRA, 2025).

        In this study, the evaluation combined four elements. Point-forecast metrics were RMSE, MAE and MAPE, with MAPE adopted as the primary metric due to its scale invariance. The Ljung-Box test at lag 10 verified the ARIMA in-sample residuals. The significance tests were the Friedman test for the omnibus comparison, the Wilcoxon signed-rank with Bonferroni correction for pairwise comparisons, and the Diebold-Mariano test with the Harvey et al. (1997) small-sample correction, used as the primary instrument for ranking the three algorithms. SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values were computed through the TreeExplainer algorithm of the shap library (LUNDBERG; LEE, 2017), generated independently for Random Forest and LightGBM. A composite exchange rate sensitivity metric, the mean absolute SHAP value of FX and REER features averaged between the two models, was visualized as a heatmap across the 24 country-sector combinations.

## 5.6 Deployment

        Deployment is the CRISP-DM phase covering all activities in an operational environment: packaging code, integrating it into a system, and establishing real-time infrastructure with continuous monitoring of quality and effectiveness (SHIMAOKA et al., 2024).

        This is the final and ongoing phase of the model lifecycle, ensuring that models work technically and continuously bring value to the business (TRIPATHI et al., 2021). It involves converting predictions and insights into usable artifacts, such as dashboards, periodic reports or automated notifications, delivering information in an understandable and actionable form for stakeholders (PODDAR et al., 2024).

        A theoretical prediction pipeline includes data ingestion and preprocessing, feature engineering, model building, model evaluation, and persistence and publication of results, with standard components such as ETL and API deployment (RASCHKA et al., 2020; PODDAR et al., 2024). Best practices for traceability and auditability, such as log storage and change management (NOGARE; SILVEIRA, 2024), and data versioning (PEIKERT; BRANDMAIER, 2021), support experimental reproducibility.

        Documentation and versioning are essential for traceability and transparency throughout the model lifecycle. Code versioning uses systems such as Git and collaborative platforms like GitHub (SEMMELROCK et al., 2024). The ability to easily revert changes fosters experimentation and innovation by reducing the perceived risk of adopting new approaches (STROMER et al., 2024).

        Although a production-grade forecasting service was outside the scope of this academic study, the Deployment phase of CRISP-DM was operationalized through the publication of a reproducible artifact set. The complete pipeline (raw and processed datasets, feature engineering scripts, trained models, hyperparameter configurations, forecast outputs and evaluation notebooks) was organized in a public Git repository with versioned commits, treating reproducibility as the primary deployment deliverable.
