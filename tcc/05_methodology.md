# 5 METHODOLOGY

        In this research, the Cross-Industry Standard Process for Data Mining (CRISP-DM) was used to predict the impacts of changes in exchange rates on trade flows between United States and Canada, Mexico, and Brazil. This decision was motivated by the need to connect with macroeconomic database, time series data and predictive models.

        The methodology of CRISP-DM is widely recognized as one of the most commonly used methodologies in data mining projects, which organizes the project into well-defined phases. That structure guarantees the quality and transparency of the models at all stages, from business understanding to deployment, enabling the generation of insights and implementation analytical solutions (TRIPATHI et al., 2021).

        The process consists of six phases that are the focus of this chapter: Business Understanding, Data Understanding, Data Preparation, Modeling, Evaluation and Deployment (WIRTH; HIPP, 2000). Although the phases are presented sequentially in the subsections that follow, the actual research process involved repeated cycles between Data Preparation and Modeling, and between Modeling and Evaluation, consistent with the iterative nature of the framework.

## 5.1 Business Understanding

        This is the first phase of the CRISP-DM methodology, where the project's goals are set and the required actions are determined. At this stage, requirements are transformed to a definition of the data mining problem and project plans defining activities for several subsequent steps (SHIMAOKA et al., 2024). This stage also covers enlightenment in the economic and institutional environment, identification of external forces like exchange rate volatility, global macroeconomic shock and structural asymmetry. These factors are very important because they affect both analytical models and the interpretation of the results, and they should be included in policy making and whenever we evaluate global impact (NGALAWA; KUTU, 2017).

        To ensure that the results of the forecasting model are directly useable for strategic business decisions, a profound knowledge of the business objectives was necessary in order to adjust expectations. This initial comprehension covered from theoretically mapping the influence on trade flows and currency exchange variations to uncovering relevant actors, including potential users of the model such as stakeholders, policy and decision makers. Methodologically, business understanding in the first alignment phase was important for calling into question what aspects of the economy should be given greater depth of analysis later on. These features encompassed the sectoral sensitivity analysis and export-import variation, as well as the effect of macroeconomic incentives on trade flow decisions, an important consideration for shaping the understanding of trading patterns and policymaking (BATARSEH et al., 2020).

        In this study, the Business Understanding phase consisted in determining the most important macroeconomic variables, defining performance metrics to evaluate how well predictive models perform and, very importantly, clearly stating the core problem to be solved. In this respect, the global purpose of the project was to assess the accuracy of Random Forest and LightGBM compared to ARIMA forecast when predicting trade flows between the United States and Canada, Mexico, and Brazil, based on the currency fluctuations rule, from the year 2000 to 2025, that dictated the initial definition of project features. The choice of these variables and metrics was not arbitrary; it came directly from the prior review of theory and what is known about traditional models such as ARIMA being limited for complex macroeconomic time series, and the potential of machine learning methods to model nonlinear relationships (LIN et al., 2020).

        Business Understanding structured the remaining stages of the methodology in order to face the specific research objectives: identifying the sectors most vulnerable to exchange rate changes; analyzing how these effects vary over different time horizons; evaluating the relative predictive power of each model considered; and examining the role of macroeconomic factors in the volatility of trade flows.

## 5.2 Data Understanding

        The Data Understanding phase, as it appears in methodologies like CRISP-DM, is the time when data is described, explored, and its quality and availability are checked. This process becomes the basis for a plan for future transformations. For recognizing patterns, discovering limitations and hypothesis-making, the raw data is collected and analyzed. This phase is often exploratory and concerned with assessing data quality, which underpins the success of experiments and the trustworthiness of information (RIESS, 2022).

        The kind of data for analysis and prediction models is usually composed of historical exchange rate data taken from worldwide financial markets or central bank respondents, as well as macroeconomic indicators like interest, inflation, GDP growth, trade balance, and foreign direct investment obtained from sources such as the World Bank (WB), International Monetary Fund (IMF) or national statistical bureaus (ABIR et al., 2024). In this analysis, these encompassed historical series of USD/CAD, USD/MXN, and USD/BRL exchange rates taken from central banks and the Federal Reserve (FED), bilateral trade flows retrieved from databases such as UN Comtrade or U.S. Census Bureau, general macroeconomic indicators from the World Bank or Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) as well as sectoral data sources such as commodities, manufactured goods, and high tech goods useful to capture the sectoral asymmetry of trade flows.

        The frequency of this data ranges from monthly to quarterly, depending on the existence and consistency of the series. This flexibility was essential for adjusting the model to specific time horizons of analysis and enabled an understanding both of short-term effects and long-term structural activities (ABOUZAID; BOUSSEDRA, 2025). Data manipulation and integration for analysis occurred using the Python programming language with packages including Pandas and NumPy used for data loading and processing (BATARSEH et al., 2020).

        Additionally, during the early stage of data exploration, descriptive statistics such as mean, median, and standard deviation are computed to understand data distribution and relationships between variables (LI et al., 2025). This process also enables the assessment of data quality and guides the cleaning and transformation procedures required to ensure reliable analyses and adequate representation of macroeconomic and sectoral aspects (VERSTRAETE et al., 2021).

        The data collected for this study covered the period from 2000 to 2024 at multiple frequencies (daily for exchange rates, monthly for macroeconomic variables and bilateral trade flows). Although collection extended back to 2000 to maximize potential coverage of post-Global Financial Crisis dynamics, the modeling window was restricted to 2010–2024 to align with the availability of monthly Comtrade data, yielding 180 monthly observations. The 24 target series were defined along three dimensions — country (Canada, Mexico, Brazil), direction (exports, imports), and sector (commodities, manufactured goods, high-technology, total) — producing the structured 3 × 2 × 4 evaluation design used in the subsequent phases. The detailed exploratory artifacts are reported in Appendix B.

## 5.3 Data Preparation

        After the data has been collected and made sense, the Data Preparation phase aims to ensure the quality and suitability of the raw data, guaranteeing that it is properly cleaned, transformed, and formatted. This includes rectifying errors and inconsistencies, treating missing values, re-coding data types to a standard format and normalization, standardization or coding of the variables for their appropriate use in model building actions. Models like ARIMA, Random Forest and LightGBM, which this paper addresses, require careful data treatment to ensure optimized performance and valid predictions. As mentioned in (MA et al., 2024), the adequacy and quality of input data are essential indicators of the success and reliability of model results.

        Screening variables is an important aspect in the process of data preparation, aiming to identify and retain only relevant features that contribute to the model's prediction and performance (MA et al., 2024). Macroeconomic variables, such as exchange rates, GDP, interest rates, exports and imports, and trade sectors are important factors to be considered in studies about economic dynamics and international trade.

        Integration of several databases was necessary to create a unified dataset that contains consistent and complete information needed for modeling. Moreover, the data frequency conversion was essential for the adjustment of models like ARIMA, which may require a certain temporal consistency for optimization of predictions (BRYAN et al., 2023). At the same time, data standardization was essential to make analysis results comparable and valid (ABIR et al., 2025).

        Outlier detection and processing, which consists of identifying atypical observations, is significant as these observations without treatment may mislead statistical results and prediction accuracy (TAJMOUATI et al., 2022). The identification of outliers was carried out by using descriptive statistical methods that summarize information on attributes, including the use of the quartile range, which detects values that fall far away from the upper and lower quartiles, and by visual inspection of graphs (SUDJIANTO; ZHANG, 2024). Nevertheless, automatic exclusion of outlier data is generally not recommended; sometimes it may be more reasonable to include outliers that will be helpful for checking the model robustness, rather than claiming about invalid data (DIAS et al., 2020).

        Differentiation is a widely used method in time series analysis, especially in econometric models where trends and seasonality have to be eliminated, thereby stabilizing the series mean and addressing non-stationarity (LIN et al., 2020). Data Preparation also includes feature engineering for building the performance and predictability of ML models, generating and transforming new variables from existing ones to make data more meaningful (CHOWDHURY et al., 2024). A subset of time series transformations is introduced, in particular lags, moving averages and percentage variations, which aid in grasping temporal behavior that is indispensable for econometric and machine learning models (LIN et al., 2020).

        The Data Preparation pipeline for this study was implemented in the `03_data_preparation.ipynb` notebook. Daily exchange rate series were aggregated to monthly frequency by simple mean; quarterly GDP series were forward-filled to a monthly cadence. Two parallel target representations were generated to accommodate the heterogeneous statistical assumptions of the three modeling families: a log-transformed variant used by Random Forest and LightGBM, and a log-differenced variant used by ARIMA. Stationarity of the differenced series was confirmed at the 5% significance level through the Augmented Dickey-Fuller test for the majority of the 24 series. Outliers were identified through the interquartile range criterion and retained in the dataset, complemented by binary regime indicators for the Global Financial Crisis (September 2008–June 2009) and the COVID-19 pandemic (March 2020–June 2021). Feature engineering generated lagged variants of the macroeconomic controls (1, 3, 6, and 12 months), rolling moving averages (3, 6, and 12 months), monthly percentage change transformations, and calendar-based indicators. The final analytical dataset comprised 180 monthly observations across 97 columns (73 features and 24 target representations). The dataset was split chronologically into a training window (January 2010 to December 2021) and an out-of-sample test window (January 2022 to December 2024, 36 months), preserving the temporal ordering of the observations and avoiding look-ahead bias (VERSTRAETE et al., 2021).

## 5.4 Modeling

        The process flow for Modeling, as an immediate continuation of Data Preparation, is next after preparing and engineering features, as outlined in the CRISP-DM methodology. At this stage, techniques like statistical tests and machine learning algorithms are executed iteratively to construct, select and refine predictive models used to detect patterns in the data (CHOWDHURY et al., 2024; MA et al., 2024). In this paper, both econometric models like ARIMA and machine learning models such as Random Forest and LightGBM were used and compared to take advantage of their time series forecasting ability.

        In general, the modeling approach in time series prediction problems involves a sequence of steps: the definition and preparation of features for assembling a supervised model; finding a prediction window essential to the predictive goal; the division of the data into training and test sets to assess how well the model's performance generalizes on unseen data; model tuning, in which hyperparameters are adjusted through cross-validation to ensure that the derived models generalize well; comparative evaluation based on metrics; and explainability analysis of the model to gain higher performance and confidence (SILVA; TIMO, 2022). Avoiding data leakage, the so-called "contamination zone" effect, is important because inconsistency between the training and testing phases can lead to erroneous assessments of model performance (REMIL et al., 2024).

        The way to define time series models as inputs is varied, and ARIMA models are widely used for univariate time series (PORTO, 2022). For Random Forest and LightGBM, both of which are models based on decision trees, the data input can be multivariate and does not require time series stationarity. This flexibility allowed them to more easily incorporate several macroeconomic lagged variables (BOTZ et al., 2025), as well as features derived through feature engineering — such as lags, moving averages and calendar features — all of which are pivotal for capturing temporal and seasonal dynamics in the data (ZANOTTI, 2025).

        The AutoRegressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) model has been used as a benchmark method for time series analysis, such as financial and economic time series. Its approach involves the removal of non-stationarity, primarily through differentiation, to enable modeling the time series (LIN et al., 2020). Its parameterization (p, d, q) models autoregressive dependencies (p), the level of differentiation required to remove trends (d), and the moving average component (q) (RIZVI, 2024). ARIMA has been employed as a fundamental baseline model for comparative forecasting performance studies, and in certain applications, it can even emerge as the best approach for short-term forecasts, serving as an effective baseline for the evaluation of more complex models (BOTZ et al., 2025).

        For each single-variable time series, the standard modeling practice started with a stationarity test, for which the Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) test was applied to determine the order of differentiation (d) of the series. The final model selection was then performed using information criteria such as the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), with the goal of identifying the minimum values to detect the most parsimonious and best-fitted models. A rigorous diagnostic analysis of the residuals was crucial to confirm the absence of heteroscedasticity and autocorrelation, which are White Noise properties, thereby validating the model's assumptions and ensuring its adequacy (REICHERT; SOUZA, 2020). The backtesting process for ARIMA models occurred retrospectively using a sliding window approach, with models trained on historical data up to a point in time and predictions generated for the following period (BOTZ et al., 2025).

        Random Forest is implemented using an ensemble approach which aggregates multiple decision trees over the supervised matrix, as it is applied in prediction tasks (ABIR et al., 2025). Unlike ARIMA, it performs well for non-linear relationships and is capable of capturing complex interactions between predictors without assuming the underlying data to be stationary (CHATTERJEE et al., 2024). The aforementioned algorithm is tolerant of various data types and less sensitive to outliers and multicollinearity, which is an advantage for dealing with macroeconomic and financial data (LING; WANG, 2024; SALMAN et al., 2024).

        Similar to Random Forest, Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM) is a powerful time series forecasting algorithm. This specific algorithm is known for its speed and accuracy, often demonstrating superior performance in processing large, high-dimensional datasets (ZANOTTI, 2025). It is also based on a supervised matrix, but it requires caution in the optimization of hyperparameters and the treatment of missing values (MORTEZANEJAD; WANG, 2025).

        Nevertheless, the employment of well-tuned machine learning models for financial market prediction, though appealing, should be treated with caution due to uncertainty about how well such models can generalize across diverse market conditions and the need for thorough sensitivity analyses to validate model stability. Besides, the risk of overfitting should also be considered to ensure effectiveness in real-world scenarios and durability of the algorithms across different historical periods (WANG; XIE, 2024).

        Temporal validation is key in time series analysis because it maintains the temporal order of the data, avoids information leakage from future to past, and ensures model evaluation under realistic forecasting settings (MORTEZANEJAD; WANG, 2025). To minimize problems of improper evaluation and over-generalization, time series should be strictly split according to chronological order (KHAN et al., 2024). This preserves the temporal structure and prevents access to future data during training, thereby mimicking the natural flow of time and consequently avoiding data leakage (KHAN et al., 2024; SAFAROV et al., 2025). Because of this structure, this approach employs backtesting strategies based on sliding window methods, often referred to as time series cross-validation (TS-CV), which enables iterative evaluation of model performance, thereby ensuring that both training and test data preserve chronological order during validations (ABIR et al., 2024).

        In Machine Learning model development, hyperparameter tuning is essential for maximizing performance, with various techniques being applied (VRANJEŠ; NIGGEMANN, 2024). The most common methodologies include Grid Search, Random Search, and Optuna, which involves training a probabilistic model relating hyperparameters to the model's performance metrics. Each methodology has its unique characteristics that impact the efficiency and scope of the hyperparameter search space (RASCHKA et al., 2020; LING; WANG, 2024).

        The Modeling phase implementation, documented in the `04_modeling.ipynb` notebook, produced a total of 72 forecasting models — the factorial combination of 24 target series and 3 algorithmic families — supplemented by a Naïve random-walk baseline. ARIMA models were fitted under a walk-forward protocol with order selection by AIC grid search over p ∈ {0, 1, 2, 3}, d ∈ {0, 1}, and q ∈ {0, 1, 2, 3}. Random Forest and LightGBM were configured as direct 36-step forecasters on the logarithmic target representation, with hyperparameter optimization performed via Optuna (AKIBA et al., 2019) over 30 trials per series using a TimeSeriesSplit cross-validator with 5 folds. The Naïve baseline was implemented as log(y_t) = log(y_{t-1}); its structural information advantage relative to the direct 36-step machine learning forecasters is discussed in Section 6.2 and Section 7.3.

## 5.5 Evaluation

        In the CRISP-DM methodology, the evaluation phase is essential to verify that the models developed not only meet the business objectives previously defined in the Business Understanding phase, but are also sufficiently tuned for deployment (SATRI et al., 2023). Model validation is achieved by scrutinizing the performance, interpretability, and robustness metrics of a model to confirm its credibility and utility in decision-making (SUDJIANTO; ZHANG, 2024).

        To compare the models, the assessment of predictive accuracy and practical relevance requires the use of robust statistical metrics, such as the Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), the Mean Absolute Error (MAE), and the Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE), all of which are frequently employed to gauge prediction accuracy (AYITEY et al., 2023). Performance measures are necessary for the purpose of comparing machine learning models to each other and to traditional approaches like ARIMA to determine which model is most effective for a specific application. These evaluations are essential to quantify the value added by complex models over baseline methods and also validate the dependability and generalizability of the model (UDDIN et al., 2025).

        Although machine learning-based models may provide superior performance, their interpretability is often difficult to achieve, particularly with financial applications where the clarity of the decision-making process and transparency are a necessity (SUDJIANTO; ZHANG, 2024). To circumvent this opacity, interpretability methods such as feature importance analysis and SHAP-based explainers are applied. These methods elucidate the contribution of each input variable to the model's predictions, which facilitates a deeper understanding of its decision-making process and the individual impact of features on the outcomes (GUO et al., 2024). This enables policymakers and financial analysts to understand not only "what" is predicted, but also "why" certain predictions are generated. Overcoming the opacity of "black boxes" is crucial for cultivating trust and the adoption of these predictive tools (ABOUZAID; BOUSSEDRA, 2025). In addition, the model's interpretability is crucial for users such as policymakers and investors to understand its forecasting logic and make informed decisions; this indirectly validates the coherence of its relationships with economic indicators (KHAN et al., 2024).

        Robustness tests and sensitivity analysis are the methods used to check the validity and performance of a model across a variety of market conditions (SUDJIANTO; ZHANG, 2024). These procedures are also essential in providing systematic assessments of model stability, including analysis under various market conditions, adaptability to simulated shocks and extreme events, and resilience during high-volatility periods (ABOUZAID; BOUSSEDRA, 2025).

        The Evaluation phase implementation, documented in the `05_evaluation.ipynb` notebook, combined point-forecast metrics (RMSE, MAE, MAPE — with MAPE adopted as the primary headline metric due to its scale invariance), residual diagnostics (Ljung-Box test applied to the in-sample ARIMA residuals at lag 10), statistical significance tests (Friedman test as omnibus comparison; Wilcoxon signed-rank with Bonferroni correction for pairwise comparisons; Diebold-Mariano test with the small-sample correction proposed by Harvey et al. (1997) as the primary instrument for ranking the three algorithms), and model interpretability via SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values computed through the TreeExplainer algorithm of the `shap` library (LUNDBERG; LEE, 2017). SHAP values were generated independently for Random Forest and LightGBM, and a composite exchange rate sensitivity metric was constructed as the mean absolute SHAP value of the exchange rate and REER features averaged between the two machine learning models, visualized as a heatmap across the 24 country-sector combinations.

## 5.6 Deployment

        Deployment is the phase in the CRISP-DM methodology which includes all activities to be performed in an operational environment, including packaging the code and integrating it into a system such that the organization can use it. It includes establishing real-time infrastructure and continually monitoring quality and effectiveness in a rapidly changing environment (SHIMAOKA et al., 2024).

        This is the final and ongoing phase of the model lifecycle, whose aim is not only to ensure that developed models work technically but also to bring continuous value to the business (TRIPATHI et al., 2021). This involves converting predictions and insights into accessible and usable artifacts for end users, such as dashboards for analysts, visual notifications on existing systems, periodic reports to managers, or automated notifications to operations, ensuring that information is delivered in a manner that is understandable and actionable for various stakeholders (PODDAR et al., 2024).

        A theoretical prediction pipeline should involve a structured process that includes, but is not restricted to: Data ingestion and preprocessing, Feature engineering, Model building, Model evaluation, and the Persistence and publication of results, including standard components such as Extract, Transform, Load (ETL) and API deployment (RASCHKA et al., 2020; PODDAR et al., 2024). Every step must apply best practices that enable the traceability and auditability of the process, such as mandatory log storage and change management (NOGARE; SILVEIRA, 2024). Data versioning is fundamental for the reproducibility of experiments, reinforcing the importance of recording detailed information at every stage of the model lifecycle (PEIKERT; BRANDMAIER, 2021).

        Documentation and versioning are essential for traceability and transparency throughout the entire model lifecycle, allowing for the understanding of its operation and the audit of its decisions. In relation to code versioning, systems such as Git and collaborative platforms like GitHub are used (SEMMELROCK et al., 2024). The possibility to easily revert any changes made is a key factor to foster experimentation and innovation, as it reduces the perceived risk involved in adopting new approaches (STROMER et al., 2024).

        Although the deployment of a production-grade forecasting service was outside the scope of this academic study, the Deployment phase of CRISP-DM was operationalized through the publication of a fully reproducible artifact set. The complete pipeline — including raw and processed datasets, feature engineering scripts, trained model objects, hyperparameter configurations, forecast outputs, and evaluation notebooks — was organized in a public Git repository with versioned commits. Reproducibility was treated as the primary deployment deliverable, supporting independent verification and extension by future research.
